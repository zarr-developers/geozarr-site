## 3. The coords convention (Christophe)

`coords` treats a coordinate as a value defining a position along an array dimension (the second row corresponding to a specific time or altitude, say). It is domain-agnostic, covering time, spectral, vertical, indexing, distance, and angular dimensions in any field (scientific grids, image and signal processing, finance, manufacturing), works consistently with Multiscales (each level can define its own coordinates), and is designed to compose with existing and future conventions. Its main property maps coordinate names to coordinate descriptors so readers can discover an array's coordinates; by default a coordinate name maps to one dimension name. Coordinates can be defined on an array or shared across arrays, similar to NetCDF coordinate variables; hierarchy and root-group rules are not yet worked out. Four descriptor types:

- `array`: the standard representation, a path to an explicit coordinate array;
- `inline`: small coordinate lists embedded directly in metadata (RGB or a few spectral bands);
- `interval`: regularly spaced coordinates as start, end, and step, numeric or ISO 8601;
- `reference`: delegation to another convention by identifier, for example X and Y referencing the `spatial` convention with its affine-georeferencing requirements.

A recently added `indexed_by` supports advanced models (point clouds, trajectories) where one or more coordinates attach to one or more dimensions, the analogue of CF auxiliary coordinates, with an equivalent in CoverageJSON.

In questions: Christophe saw no use case for describing only a subset of an array's axes, since the convention replaces implicit xarray-style discovery. Michael Sumner found the framing clarifying against the persistent confusion around rectilinear and curvilinear terminology, but challenged whether `inline` differs materially from an explicit array; after Patrick's illustration (a monthly climatology carrying twelve month labels inline instead of a separate 12-by-1 string array), Christophe allowed that `inline` is a very specific case that could be dropped. Christophe will share the slides via a Medium post, which also carries the accompanying text.

## 4. The CS convention (Patrick)

CS, for coordinate set, is based on the OGC referencing-by-coordinates standard, with the perspective inverted for Zarr: the convention is built around producing the coordinates for an array, and what results is, in the standard's terms, a coordinate set. The abstract standard is filled in using CF semantics, three decades of compiled knowledge, while deliberately stepping away from CF's NetCDF encoding. Three worked examples:

- **CMIP6 (simple).** About 30 lines describe the full coordinate system of a daily file: regular latitude/longitude axes as a start plus a step (the four parameters of a non-skewed, non-rotated geotransform recoverable from the two spatial axes), and a separate temporal coordinate system using a no-leap calendar; all CF model calendars are supported. The spatial/temporal separation mirrors how OGC composes CRSs.
- **EOPF (redundancy).** EOPF's flat, NetCDF-3-style hierarchy forces duplication of coordinate data across groups; CS permits references to arrays in other groups, removing the duplication.
- **ROMS.** Curvilinear grids, parametric vertical coordinates, and model calendars are all expressible.

In questions: parametric vertical coordinates carry CF's nine formulations and formula terms, describing the dataset rather than processing it, just as a CRS identifier does not itself project data (Ethan). Axis directions use OGC vocabulary (north, east) rather than CF's pseudo-units (degrees_north). Grouping coordinates per CRS follows the OGC structure and lets, for example, the spatial subsystem of a four-dimensional compound system carry its own EPSG identifier (Max questioned the ergonomics; Patrick defended the alignment). Michael noted that compressing regular sequences to start-plus-step does not exist in today's CF, which Patrick agreed speaks for both his and Christophe's proposals; Ethan mentioned CF's newer subsampled-coordinates mechanism (tie points with interpolation, akin to ground control points) and confirmed cell boundaries are supported regularly or via a separate array, as in CF. Ethan also gave the strongest use case for separately referencable coordinate components: numerical weather prediction output with hundreds of variables sharing one horizontal definition but differing vertical and time coordinates.

Two exchanges deserve the record. Max pressed on the design goal of following OGC structures, stating frankly that most implementers he talks to find OGC standards hard to use in real software, and asked what software demonstrates value from the CRS grouping; Patrick answered that **CS is not implemented anywhere yet, being too new, with an R implementation in progress by him**. Patrick's broader position: the complexity is in the data, not the convention (the CMIP6 description is short; ROMS is long because ROMS is complex); a convention that must be understood on first read will never serve the ROMS community, and EOPF will not overcome its design inefficiencies with it; complexity is something to manage, not avoid. Ethan agreed the complexity of OGC and CF exists for reasons that are too easily dismissed.

## 5. Positions and the path forward

Christophe, noting he participates on behalf of the European Space Agency (which is adopting the Proj, Spatial, and Multiscales conventions through the Copernicus/EOPF ecosystem), recalled that mapping CF and NetCDF into Zarr has been attempted since 2023 without ever reaching consensus, which is what led to the philosophy of small, domain-agnostic pieces extended by geospatial conventions. His questions to the group: does GeoZarr want multiple ways to represent the same information, or reconciliation? And should CF be split into smaller independent conventions (a CF-properties convention, additional coordinate types) so users read only what they need? He also observed that CS reads less as an alternative to `coords` than as an alternative to `spatial` plus `proj` as well, since it captures the CRS and the affine representation; Patrick accepted the description of CS as a superset of `spatial` and `coords`, with `spatial` serving a large but restricted use case.

Max synthesized: V1 (Multiscales, Spatial, Proj) supports a limited slice of geospatial data, both proposals respond to that real gap, and the underlying problem is that GeoZarr has never defined exactly what realm of geospatial data it supports or the process for expanding it.

**Decided:** following Chris Little's earlier suggestion, the next month is spent updating the SWG charter to lay out scope and design goals (including whether to align with OGC structures, and whether simple use cases should only require reading simple components), returning to the convention designs afterwards. Patrick said yes to the charter and advocated a big-tent model in which multiple conventions doing similar things stand side by side; Max leans toward one recommended way to do a thing; Ethan's counterpoint, quoting an original CF author, is that standards development is about saying no and restricting possibilities until everyone can agree. The disagreement is to be worked through on GitHub during the charter update.
